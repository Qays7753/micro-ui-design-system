## Make A Perfect UX Case Study In 8 Steps
- source: Kole Jain · https://youtu.be/t7mpEDXzjCg
- type: principles
- tags: case-study, portfolio, presenting, copywriting, charts, imagery, prototyping, workflow

### Takeaways
- A case study has two jobs:
  - show the problem you found and how you solved it;
  - more quietly, show a client or employer what they will get if they hire you.
  All eight steps serve those two goals.
- Nail the problem before anything else. If the problem is wrong or doesn't exist, the solution is worthless however good it looks.
- A good problem statement is one plain sentence: who, what they want, and the tension. His example: New York college students who want to spend less on eating out while eating more healthily. Research then backs it up.
- Put a short solution summary at the top, straight under the problem. Readers shouldn't have to scroll to the bottom to find the outcome. The process and the full solution follow further down.
- Research can be light. A Google Form sent to friends and family is a valid start.
  - Cluster the raw data in an affinity map, turn the clusters into common findings, and let those findings become the problem statement.
  - Then show a short summary of the findings.
- Presentation matters as much as content. A case study that looks bad doesn't get read, however good the design work inside it.
- Lean on images and GIFs, and keep the text concise. Case studies tend to be text-heavy, and visuals carry understanding better.
- Data visuals: use clear charts, and circle and annotate the point you want seen. Every chart gets a title, axis titles and a legend.
- A persona exists to consolidate your data and inform design decisions. If it has no bearing on the final design, it is wasted.
  - Build it from your research and make it realistic for this problem and solution.
  - Cut details that don't relate to the problem.
  - Centre it on goals and frustrations, plus behaviour, interests and pain points.
  - Don't spend time choosing a photo or a name.
- User flows: keep them small. You don't need a big project for a good case study. Smaller scope usually means more quality per piece, and giant flows are not a mark of quality.
- Colour-code flow shapes by role: orange for a decision, blue for a process the user carries out, green for an input or output. The exact colours are shown on screen, not stated.
- Include lo-fi wireframes or sketches, hand-drawn or in Figma. They let you try layouts quickly and find the best one.
  - Showing rough or unfinished sketches (stray arrows, shaded areas) usually adds value, because it shows how you think.
  - The creator bets many people skip this step (his opinion).
- Lo-fi is also the cheap place to test assumptions before you build a full prototype.
  - Put a couple of people through the wireframes and watch whether it feels intuitive.
  - Tweak, test again, then move to the final design.
- Final design: put each design next to text that explains why you made those choices. The reasoning is what readers spend the most time on. Clickable tabs are one way to lay this out.
- Show screens as large as reasonably possible. When a study has many screens they often get shrunk, and then the detail that justifies your decisions can't be seen.
- Always include a reflection section; it is often left out. It covers:
  - what could be better, and what you would add next time;
  - a debrief of how the project went and the difficulties you hit;
  - what you learned;
  - features or sections you cut for time.
- Admitting your flaws and showing you are already improving makes you look like a better designer, even though it exposes gaps.
- A typical honest point for the reflection: early research uses friends and family, which isn't a diverse or representative sample. That is fine, but say you would improve the method.
- Keep the conclusion brief. Quality beats quantity there and across the whole study.
- Style 1, story: people dislike reading but love a story. If you worked out of order, needed an unusual method or got stuck, let that shape the narrative. His example: stuck on a user flow until a friend's comment while using the prototype sparked the answer.
- Style 2, short: think of the case study as a web page. Limit text in favour of video, images and other media. It should be readable in one sitting, about 10–15 minutes.
- Style 3, skimmable: give sections chapter-style titles, like book chapters, that sum up the content and make people curious, not generic labels.

### Recipes
1. Case-study page order [00:31–10:17]
   1. Problem statement, one research-backed sentence, followed directly by a short solution snapshot. [00:31–01:33]
   2. Research: the method (e.g. a Google Form), then the affinity map, common findings and a summary of findings. His example's findings were social motivations, priority on health and existing efforts. [02:04]
   3. A persona drawn from those findings: goals, frustrations, behaviour, interests and pain points, all tied to the problem. [03:36–04:38]
   4. A small, colour-coded user flow in FigJam. [04:38–05:40]
   5. Lo-fi wireframes or sketches, the quick usability test and the tweaks it led to. [05:40–06:43]
   6. Final design: large screens, each with explanatory text beside it, optionally in clickable tabs. [06:43–07:14]
   7. Reflection: improvements, debrief, difficulties, learnings and deferred features, then a brief conclusion. [07:14–08:45]
   8. A style pass over the whole thing: add a story thread, cut text in favour of visuals, write chapter-style headings, and check it reads in 10–15 minutes. [08:45–10:17]

   1. Pick a clear chart form.
   2. Circle or annotate the insight.
   3. Add a chart title, axis titles and a legend.

   - Use different shapes and colours for different step types: orange for a decision, blue for a user process, green for an input or output. Hex values are shown on screen, not stated.

### Gotchas
- Building on a misidentified or non-existent problem.
- Hiding the solution at the bottom of the page.
- Strong design work in a case study that looks bad or is a wall of text.
- Charts with no title, axis titles or legend.
- Personas full of irrelevant detail, or ones that never influence the design. Time spent on the persona's photo or name.
- Huge user flows and over-scoped projects.
- Skipping lo-fi, or leaving out sketches because they look messy.
- Shrinking screens until the detail can't be seen.
- No reflection section.
- A long conclusion, or a study that can't be read in one sitting.
- Dull, generic section titles.

## Figma Update: Everything In Under 2 Minutes
- source: Kole Jain · https://youtu.be/fL1X2Mw6s3w
- type: tool-tutorial
- tags: figma, auto-layout, workflow

### Takeaways
- The update is multi-edit. You select similar elements across several frames and change them together, and each change lands as if you had edited every element on its own. Before this you edited them one by one, which he calls a real pain.
- Figma can find the matching layers for you with one button. It matches on layer names and on where the layer sits in its grouping structure.
- Text works too: select the matching text layers, then type once to change them all.
- Auto layout can be applied across frames: select the elements in each frame and press Shift+A.
- With matching children selected in several auto layouts, moving one moves it in all of them. You reorder once and the change applies everywhere.
- Sections set the scope. Only frames inside the section are included in the multi-edit.

### Recipes
1. Bulk-edit repeated elements [00:00–00:30]
   1. Select one element.
   2. Press the button that grabs all matching layers (shown on screen, not stated). Figma names this "Select matching layers"; the video doesn't say the name.
   3. Change properties once.
2. Bulk-edit text [00:30]
   1. Select a text layer.
   2. Press the two on-screen buttons the video shows (not named).
   3. Type the new text; every match updates.
3. Auto layout across frames [00:30]
   - Select the elements in each frame and press Shift+A.
4. Reorder across auto layouts [00:30–01:00]
   - With the matching element selected in every auto layout, move it once.
5. Limit the scope [01:00]
   - Put the target frames inside a Section. Multi-edit then affects only frames within it.

### Gotchas
- Matching keys off layer names and grouping position, so inconsistently named or structured layers won't be picked up. This is an implication of the stated matching rule.
- Without a section around them, other frames on the page can be pulled into the edit.

## Developing Premium Load animations (HTML, CSS & JS): Part 2
- source: Kole Jain · https://youtu.be/d4MF6pdAZNw
- type: technique
- tags: animation, loading, navigation, micro-interaction, hero, typography, gsap

### Takeaways
- Three page-load animations built with plain HTML and CSS plus GSAP timelines in `app.js`. The auto-captions say "gap"; the library is GSAP.
  - a black preloader that slides away;
  - a nav that grows from a dot into a pill;
  - a text wipe.
  They recreate the Figma prototypes from Part 1.
- The same build pattern runs through all three:
  1. Style the finished layout in CSS.
  2. Put elements in their start state (opacity 0, width 0, max-width 0, offset down).
  3. Build a `gsap.timeline({ paused: true })` and chain tweens onto it.
  4. Call `.play()`.
- Shared ease: `expo.inOut`. He chose it because it gives the same feel as the custom exponential Bézier curves he drew in Figma.
- Durations:
  - large moves (preloader slide, nav stretch): 1.3 s;
  - each half of the text wipe: 1 s;
  - nav pop-in: 0.8 s;
  - inner nav fades: 0.6 s each, staggered 0.2 s;
  - nav fade after the preloader: 0.3 s.
- Preloader sequence:
  1. Wait 1 s, then fade the logo in.
  2. Slide the black screen up by its own height (`yPercent: -100`) over 1.3 s with `expo.inOut`.
  3. At the same moment, the hero text rises from 100% below its resting place to 0 over the same 1.3 s and ease.
  4. Fade the navbar in over 0.3 s.
- The content has to move with the curtain. Start the hero text pushed down by 100% of its own height so it travels up as the loader lifts.
- `yPercent` is a percentage of the element's own height, not of the viewport.
- The timeline position parameter `"<"` starts a tween at the same time as the one before it. Without it, tweens on a timeline play one after another.
- Initial hidden states can be set in CSS or with `gsap.set()`. Both work; he says each has pros and cons but doesn't go into them.
- Expanding nav:
  - Measure the bar with `gsap.getProperty(".nav-container", "height")`. It returned 27 in the video.
  - Use that number as the starting `maxWidth`. Width equal to height gives a circle.
- Expanding nav sequence:
  1. Scale 0 → 1 over 0.8 s: a dot appears and grows into the circle.
  2. `maxWidth` from the measured height to 500px over 1.3 s with `expo.inOut`: the circle stretches into a pill.
  3. The three inner groups fade in with `stagger: 0.2` and `duration: 0.6`.
- To animate several elements with one tween, pass GSAP an array of selectors plus `stagger`, instead of writing one tween per element.
- Text wipe sequence:
  1. A solid bar grows left to right over the hidden text (width 0% → 100%, 1 s, `expo.inOut`).
  2. The text is switched on underneath.
  3. The bar is re-anchored to the right (`right: 0`, `left: auto`).
  4. The bar shrinks to 0% (1 s, `expo.inOut`), uncovering the text from left to right.
- He expects you to adjust the timings by eye after the first pass.

const preloader = gsap.timeline({ paused: true });
gsap.set(".navbar", { opacity: 0 });
gsap.set(".header-spacer", { yPercent: 100 });            // 100% of its own height
preloader
  .to(".logo-container", { opacity: 1, delay: 1 })        // duration shown on screen, not stated
  .to(".loading-screen", { yPercent: -100, duration: 1.3, ease: "expo.inOut" })
  .to(".header-spacer", { yPercent: 0, duration: 1.3, ease: "expo.inOut" }, "<") // concurrent
  .to(".navbar", { opacity: 1, duration: 0.3 });

const elms = [".menu-container", ".logo-container", ".join-container"];
navAnim

  .fromTo(".nav-container", { maxWidth: navHeight },

### Gotchas
- Set the fixed loader's `z-index` (10) straight away, or other content can sit on top of it.
- The raw logo SVG is very large; constrain it (`width: 10%`).
- A wide flex container needs `justify-content: center` too, or the logo and text sit off-centre to the left.
- If the CSS `max-width` stays at 500px, the nav expansion doesn't work properly. Set it to `0px` in CSS and put 500px in the tween's end state.
- A timeline created with `paused: true` does nothing until `.play()` is called, with the brackets.
- Leaving out `"<"` makes the hero text rise after the curtain has gone, not with it.
- `yPercent` offsets are relative to the element's own height. Don't expect viewport units.
- For the wipe re-anchor, `right: 0` alone sometimes fails, so also set `left: auto`. Derived explanation: when `left`, `right` and `width` are all set, left-to-right layout honours `left`, so the CSS `left: 0` has to be cleared.
- A 100%-tall wipe bar looked too big. At 80% tall it left the text's bottom visible until it was moved down with `top: 10%`. Keep the bar slightly taller than the glyphs and centred on them.
- (Derived, not from the video) The absolutely positioned `.text-wipe` needs its text container to be positioned (`position: relative`).
- (Derived, not from the video) The starting circle is only round if the measured height and `max-width` refer to the same box. Check `box-sizing`, and note that the 30px side padding sets a minimum width.
- (Derived, not from the video) Animating `width` and `max-width` triggers layout on every frame. The transform version above avoids that.
- Expect to fine-tune the timings after the first build.

## Prototyping Professional Load Animations in Figma: Part 1
- source: Kole Jain · https://youtu.be/nl8OFGdx75w
- type: tool-tutorial
- tags: figma, prototyping, smart-animate, animation, loading, navigation, micro-interaction, typography

### Takeaways
- Three load animations prototyped in Figma, which Part 2 rebuilds in code:
  - a loading screen that slides up to reveal the page;
  - a nav bar that grows from a dot (his favourite);
  - a text wipe.
- Every state is its own duplicated frame. Change one thing per duplicate (opacity, size or position), then chain the frames with After delay triggers.
- Set things you will animate later to opacity 0 rather than removing them, so the transition can fade them.
- Use a longer first delay as the opening hold: 1000 ms for the loader, 800 ms for the nav and the wipe. After that, chain each step with After delay 1 ms so the sequence plays on its own.
- Instant is for steps that should snap: a dot appearing, the text turning on under the cover bar, the bar disappearing. Smart Animate is for steps that move.
- Easing split:
  - large movements get a custom Bézier pulled into an exponential shape: loader slide 800 ms, nav stretch 400 ms, wipe 700 ms;
  - small reveals get Ease in and out at 200–300 ms.
- Loader: before it slides, push the page content down slightly, so the content rises into place as the loader lifts. That shared movement makes the effect better.
- Nav: build the frames in reverse. Start from the finished bar, compress it to a circle (53 × 53 px, matching the bar's 53 px height), then to a 1 × 1 px dot, then to an invisible dot.
- Nav reveal: the inner items fade in one step at a time (menu, then logo, then "Join us"), each 200 ms Ease in and out.
- Text wipe: a rectangle 1 px wide and a little taller than the text, in the right colour, grows across the text. The text turns on underneath, then the rectangle collapses toward the far side and is hidden.
- Preview by setting a flow starting point on the first frame and pressing play.

### Gotchas
- Leaving the content in place under the loader makes the reveal flatter. Nudge it down so it rises with the slide.
- The circle frame only reads as a circle if width equals height (53 × 53 here).
- Make the 1 × 1 px dot transparent in the first frame. Otherwise it is visible during the 800 ms hold instead of popping in.
- Draw the wipe bar slightly taller than the text and in the intended colour, or the edges of the glyphs show.
- The nav needs every intermediate state as its own frame (dot, circle, empty bar, then one frame per item). Building backwards from the finished nav is the easy route.
- The Figma durations don't carry over one to one: the coded versions in Part 2 run longer (see the comparison table above). Re-tune after moving to code.
