## I Studied 10,000 Paywall Screens (THIS Makes People Pay)
- source: Tim Gabe · https://youtu.be/sYRhXB_ZcLI
- type: product-psychology
- tags: paywall, conversion, trust, onboarding, psychology, copywriting, growth, mobile, localization, experimentation

### Takeaways
- Scope note: this video is about paywall structure (plan mix, trial, timing, transparency, price level, testing), backed by vendor data. It does not describe plan-card visuals, which plan is pre-selected, CTA button labels or close-button treatment. The only on-screen copy it quotes is "No payment due now". Read the layout rules below as structure and order, not visual spec.
- Headline framing: across 10,000 paywalls and about $3 billion of subscription revenue, top apps earn 18.7x more revenue, and their paywalls 636% more LTV, than average. He attributes this to six behaviours, not to better products or categories (creator's claim, unverified). Later he ties the 18.7x to experiment count (pattern 6) and the 636% to adding a trial (pattern 3).
- Pattern 1, weekly is the new monthly:
  - Two years ago weekly plans earned 43% of subscription app revenue; now 55.6%. Monthly fell from 21.1% to 11.7% in the same window (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Weekly plans convert 1.7–7.4x better than annual plans at every price tier (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Why it works: a user with high intent but unsure of long-term value will commit to about $7 a week but not to a year up front, even though annual is a far better deal for them. His framing: ask for a coffee, not a calendar year.
  - He quotes the annual as "$38 a year" and, a sentence later, "$400 a year". At $7 a week the second figure is the consistent one.
  - Many weekly starters stay. By about month four they are invested enough that upgrading to annual is easy.
  - Rule: don't default to monthly because that's what you launched with. Ask why weekly isn't your first offer.
- Pattern 2, you have one session:
  - 82% of subscription starts happen on install day (RevenueCat); Adapty puts it at 89.4% (creator's claim, unverified).
  - 55% of 3-day-trial cancellations also happen on day zero. Trial-to-paid conversion drops sharply after day 7 and never recovers (creator's claim, unverified).
  - So the first session is the whole subscription game. The winners are not the apps with the shortest paywall or onboarding.
  - Cal AI ran users through 28 onboarding screens before showing any price, and made $35M in year one (creator's claim, unverified). The onboarding-paradox video in batch-04 calls the same flow a "20-plus-step quiz". He still calls short onboarding a good rule of thumb; Cal AI just shows it is not the only way.
  - The heavy lifting inside Cal AI's flow was a three-screen trial explanation. It told the user exactly when they would be charged and explicitly promised a reminder before the trial ended. The headline was "No payment due now". By the time users reached the paywall, they had already decided.
  - Lose It ran the same play in fewer screens. A whole section of the paywall explains how the free trial works, walking through the full timeline up to the first payment. $3.3M monthly revenue at 350k installs (creator's claim, unverified).
  - He calls this one of five structures the industry treats as canonical for million-dollar paywalls. The other four are not named.
  - Rule: treat the paywall as a trust screen, not only a pricing screen. Check two things: does it appear after the user has felt something, and does it say exactly when they will be charged? If not, you are asking for trust before earning it.
- Pattern 3, structure over price:
  - Adapty's 2026 report ranks paywall experiments by measured LTV uplift. He says it covers "$3 in subscription data", probably $3 billion garbled in the captions (creator's claim, unverified):
    - localisation (paywall translated into the user's language and currency): 62.3% LTV uplift
    - trial-structure changes: 59.6%
    - plan-count changes: 63% more conversion uplift than any price test
    - price changes: far lower than all of the above
  - The number of plans you show moves results more than the price you charge. Most founders A/B test $9.99 against $12.99 for three weeks and never test plan count. He does not say which plan count wins.
  - The biggest structural finding: a weekly plan with no trial is worth $7.40 LTV over 12 months. The same weekly plan with a trial is worth $54.50. That is the 636% from the intro, and it came from structure, not price (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Yazio made around $3M a month at 700,000 installs. Its paywall was built structure-first (plan options, trial framing, streak mechanics), with price tuned last (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Rule: you lose subscribers on architecture, not on price.
- Pattern 4, honest paywalls:
  - This breaks the assumption that reducing friction means hiding the cost (fewer clicks, less information, faster commitment).
  - Blinkist's documented redesign added one thing: an explicit line saying exactly when users would be charged and for what. It sat front and centre, not in fine print. Results: conversion +23%, complaints −55%, churn unchanged (creator's claim, unverified).
  - His mechanism, "trust economics": surprised users cancel at once and review badly, while users who know what they signed up for stay. Transparency wins over the users who didn't believe the offer was real.
  - Any ambiguity about when the charge happens, what the user gets or how to cancel costs you conversions and cancellations at the same time. Transparency is a conversion mechanism, not a soft value.
- Pattern 5, higher prices, better conversion:
  - RevenueCat's 2026 data: the median high-priced app converts downloads at 2.8%, the median mid-priced app at 2%, and the top quartile of high-priced apps at 6.1% (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Mechanism: a higher price attracts higher-intent users, who decided they were serious before seeing the paywall. It does not mean any given user pays more because you charge more.
  - He explicitly does not say "raise your price tomorrow". His point is narrower: the data doesn't support assuming a lower price will save conversion. Benchmarking against the cheapest competitor is a race to the bottom.
- Pattern 6, the experimentation gap:
  - Apps running 50+ paywall experiments a year earn 18.7x more than apps running one, in the same categories at similar price points (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Top performers average almost 15 paywall experiments a year. Most founders run zero or one and treat the paywall like a checkout screen they never touch again (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Single-surface experiments compound. A home-screen widget redesign for Freecash, one of the creator's clients, lifted day-7 retention by several percentage points (creator's claim, unverified).

### Recipes
1. One-session subscription flow, assembled from patterns 2 and 4 [03:04–05:36, 07:42–08:45] (screen order and copy only; no visual values stated)
   1. Onboarding comes first. Place the paywall after the user has felt the product's value (Cal AI shows no price until after 28 screens).
   2. Before the paywall, add a trial-explanation block (three screens at Cal AI):
      - headline "No payment due now"
      - the exact date the user will be charged
      - an explicit promise of a reminder before the trial ends
   3. On the paywall, give a section to the trial timeline (Lose It), walking step by step from today to the first payment.
   4. Put a line in the main reading flow saying when the charge happens and what it pays for (Blinkist). It must not be fine print.
   5. Leave no ambiguity on the three points pattern 4 names: charge timing, what is included, how to cancel.
   6. Localise the paywall's language and currency, the top-ranked experiment.
   7. Not stated in the video: plan-card design, the pre-selected plan, CTA label, close-button placement or delay. Decide these by testing, not from this source.
2. Plan structure [01:01–03:04, 05:36–07:42]
   1. Offer weekly as the entry plan instead of defaulting to monthly.
   2. Attach a free trial to the weekly plan (the $7.40 → $54.50 LTV case).
   3. Treat annual as the later upgrade, once the user is invested (around month four).
   4. Test plan count before price.
   5. Tune price last. Yazio's order: plan options → trial framing → streak mechanics → price.
3. Paywall experiment programme [05:36–06:40, 10:17–11:50]
   1. Treat the paywall, and other single surfaces such as a home-screen widget, as a standing test surface rather than a finished screen.
   2. Cadence benchmark: top performers run almost 15 a year (creator's claim, unverified).
   3. Test in order of reported uplift: localisation, trial structure, plan count, then price.

### Gotchas
- Treating the paywall as a checkout screen you build once and never test.
- Running price tests while never testing plan count, trial structure or localisation.
- Leaving the charge date, what is bought or how to cancel vague or in fine print. Per Blinkist this costs conversions and raises complaints.
- Showing the price before the user has felt value, which asks for trust before it is earned.
- Reading pattern 5 as "charge more and everyone pays more". The creator warns against exactly that misreading.
- Copying Cal AI's 28 screens without the trial-explanation block that did the work. Batch-04's onboarding-paradox video covers when a long flow is justified.
- Ethics, weekly-first pricing: it works partly because users who would never pay about $364 a year up front ($7 × 52, derived) will pay it weekly, and the creator himself says annual is a far better deal for them. Applied ethically, the annual option and its saving stay visible and comparable on the same paywall, not hidden.
- Ethics, long pre-price onboarding: it leans on sunk effort (see batch-04's "mirror" pattern). The honest trial framing in pattern 4 is the clean counterweight and should travel with it.
- Evidence quality: the numbers come from RevenueCat and Adapty reports (subscription-tooling vendors) and the creator's agency clients. Treat them as directional.

## I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps (Here's What Actually Works)
- source: Tim Gabe · https://youtu.be/LXX_qOA5D8E
- type: product-psychology
- tags: gamification, retention, habit, psychology, micro-interaction, animation, states, dark-patterns

### Takeaways
- Note: the title says 500+ apps; in the video he says "hundreds". He promises seven research-backed patterns but announces only five by number. Patterns 2 (Strava) and 6 (Apple Watch) are numbered here from their order.
- Pattern 1, the PBL fallacy:
  - Points, badges and leaderboards (PBL) are what every app adds first. He says they are also the most documented failures in product history.
  - In 2024 LinkedIn retired its Community Top Voice gold badges. Its internal read was that badge-chasers produced quantity over quality (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Foursquare scrapped mayorships and badges in 2014. Gamification drove check-ins but not the discovery behaviour the business needed (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Google News killed its badges for the same reason (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Yu-kai Chou's framing, paraphrased: PBL is a game's scoreboard, not the game. Nobody looks at a stadium scoreboard and feels like playing baseball. Most apps build the scoreboard and forget the game.
  - Rule (derived from the LinkedIn and Foursquare cases): reward the behaviour the product actually needs, not a countable proxy for it.
- Pattern 2, engineer the size of the competition (Strava):
  - Strava has 180M registered users without a traditional badge in sight (creator's claim, unverified). Batch-04's leaderboards video gave "more than 100 million".
  - Users average 1 hour of real-world activity for every 2 minutes in the app. He calls this an influence metric rather than a retention metric (creator's claim, unverified).
  - The engine is segments: any user-defined stretch of road or trail. Run it and your time goes on a leaderboard sorted by age and gender cohort.
  - Instead of one global leaderboard, Strava runs thousands of hyper-local micro-competitions: the hill on your morning route, the segment outside your office.
  - Winnability is the strongest predictor of competitive motivation (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Kudos, the small social acknowledgement after a completed run, raise future run frequency according to a 2022 ScienceDirect study (creator's claim, unverified). Clubs grew 59% in 2024, and 14 billion kudos were given in 2025 (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Rule: let users compete locally. Design how big each competition is, rather than inflating empty metrics.
- Pattern 3, the S-curve problem:
  - A 2025 peer-reviewed study in Frontiers in Psychology found that engagement follows an S-curve against the number of gamification features. Adding features helps up to a point, then engagement reverses (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Habitica is the proof case. Daily tasks become quests, habits become character stats, and a missed task damages your HP. A peer-reviewed study found that 100% of participants had counterproductive effects: managing the game layer buried the actual productivity (creator's claim, unverified).
  - His term for it: cognitive overload disguised as engagement. If you stack streaks, points, badges, challenges and leaderboards, you are past the peak, not climbing it.
- Pattern 4, the streak trap:
  - Per the Decision Lab, the longer a streak runs, the more it shifts from motivation to obligation: "I want to do this" becomes "I can't miss today" (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Snapchat is the canonical case. A 2023 Belgian study of almost 2,500 adolescents linked streak frequency to FOMO, problematic smartphone use and lower social-media self-control (creator's claim, unverified).
  - The regulatory context he gives (creator's claim, unverified):
    - the Nevada Attorney General sued Snapchat in 2024
    - the EU Digital Fairness Act, heading towards a legislative proposal in late 2026, targets addictive streak mechanics
  - Streaks aren't always bad. Duolingo has run hundreds of experiments on its streak, and the current version retains users. The difference is agency: users choose their goal level and can buy a streak freeze.
  - The design researchers are flagging is a streak you can't pause, influence or escape.
  - In passing he says streaks "burn out at day 31", with no source (creator's claim, unverified).
- Pattern 5, variable reward magnitude (anticipation, not loss aversion):
  - The gap between knowing a reward is coming and not knowing how big it is. He calls it one of the strongest engagement signals in product design.
  - The example is Gameblazers, a fantasy card game designed by the creator's agency, where pack opening is the heart of the product. It runs in three stages, anticipation → reveal → celebration (see Recipes).
  - The contrast with streaks: the surface behaviour is the same, but the emotional engine is opposite. Streaks run on fear of losing what you built and burn out. Variable rewards run on the pull towards what comes next and keep recharging.
- Pattern 6, completion drive (Apple Watch), which he calls the most important point in the video:
  - The activity rings produced a 49.5% behaviour change across 160,000 people (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Three rings, move, exercise and stand. Closing all three completes your day.
  - The principle is Gestalt closure: the brain reads an incomplete shape as demanding completion. A ring filled to 90% is an open loop the user wants to close.
  - People who regularly close their rings are 48% less likely to have poor sleep quality (creator's claim, unverified).
  - What makes it good gamification: the mechanic produces a real-world positive outcome, not engagement theatre. The engine is anticipation towards closure, wanting to finish what you started.
- Pattern 7, competence versus badge theatre:
  - A 2024 Springer Nature meta-analysis found that gamification reliably raises perceived autonomy and relatedness but barely moves competence. Competence is the need most tied to long-term intrinsic motivation (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Most apps design for recognition and neglect mastery.
  - Peloton is the counter-example. Members who use the social and output features work out 15% more often (creator's claim, unverified), and the mechanism is competence feedback, not competition:
    - output is measured in real time
    - personal records are flagged automatically
    - the 100-ride badge means something because it stands for 100 actual rides
  - The same play elsewhere: Chess.com's Elo rating, and Garmin's training readiness and body battery scores.
  - Rule: build mechanics that show the user got better at the real thing, not that they opened the app a lot.

### Recipes
1. Hyper-local leaderboard, as narrated for Strava [02:37–04:11] (no layout values stated)
   1. Let users define small competitions tied to a real place (a segment).
   2. Log each completion's time to that segment's leaderboard automatically.
   3. Sort or filter the board by age and gender cohort so the comparison group is small and comparable.
   4. Add a lightweight social acknowledgement (kudos) on each completed activity.
2. Streak wrapped in agency, as narrated for Duolingo [06:47–07:17]
   1. Let the user set their own goal level.
   2. Offer a streak freeze (purchasable at Duolingo).
   3. Avoid the flagged version: a streak with no pause, no influence and no way out.
3. Three-stage pack opening, as narrated for Gameblazers [07:49–08:51] (no durations or easing stated)
   1. Anticipation: the user taps a pack without knowing what's inside, maybe junk, maybe the rare card they've chased for weeks.
   2. Reveal: cards flip one at a time, and each card restarts the anticipation. The same data becomes five dopamine events instead of one.
   3. Celebration: on a rare card the screen reacts with a glow and haptics, which prompts the next pack.
4. Closure rings, as narrated for Apple Watch [09:21–10:23]
   1. Show three concentric progress rings, one per daily goal (move, exercise, stand).
   2. Show partial fill continuously, so a nearly closed ring reads as an open loop.
   3. Define "day complete" as all three rings closed.
5. Competence feedback, as narrated for Peloton, Chess.com and Garmin [10:55–12:00]
   1. Show performance output live during the activity.
   2. Flag personal records automatically when they happen.
   3. Tie milestone badges to real volume (100 rides = 100 rides), never to app opens.
   4. Expose one skill rating (Elo) or readiness score (training readiness, body battery) that moves when the user actually improves.

### Gotchas
- Shipping points, badges and leaderboards first, i.e. building the scoreboard without the game.
- Rewarding a countable proxy (posts, check-ins) that drifts from the behaviour the business needs (LinkedIn, Foursquare).
- One global leaderboard instead of small, winnable cohorts.
- Stacking mechanics past the top of the S-curve until the game layer buries the real task (Habitica).
- Badges that mark app opens rather than skill or real-world volume.
- Ethics, streaks: by the creator's own account they slide into obligation, the evidence on adolescents is negative, and regulators (the Nevada AG, the EU Digital Fairness Act) are targeting them. If a streak ships, it needs goal choice, a freeze and a pause or exit.
- Ethics, variable rewards: the pack opening runs on the same mechanism as a slot machine. The video doesn't say whether Gameblazers packs cost money. Where they do, this is loot-box design and should be treated as gambling-adjacent. The creator presents the case favourably and it is his agency's client.
- Completion rings are the cleanest pattern here because closing the loop produces a real-world outcome. An open loop on a goal with no real benefit is just pressure.

## How a Chinese Unicorn App "Out-Addicts" Netflix
- source: Tim Gabe · https://youtu.be/7dCZrtH15zc
- type: product-psychology
- tags: paywall, conversion, retention, psychology, mobile, gestures, navigation, dark-patterns

### Takeaways
- Subject: ReelShort (captions say "RealShort"), a vertical short-drama app. He says it holds users longer per day than the biggest streaming services and shoots whole series in four days in a repurposed COVID hotel in China (creator's claim, unverified).
- His framing: it isn't really a streaming app but "Candy Crush with a plot", using mobile-game mechanics (virtual coin wallets, ad-gated episodes, cliffhanger paywalls). The patterns transfer to any product where users become emotionally invested.
- Pattern 1, the graduated gate:
  - The usual free-to-paid move is binary: free trial, then a hard paywall one day. Most users bounce at that wall, partly because the jump feels sudden even when they like the product.
  - ReelShort makes the transition almost imperceptible across three stages:
    - Stage 1, episodes 1–5: completely free. Content plays instantly, with no account, sign-up or email. You are watching within seconds. The product proves its worth before asking for anything.
    - Stage 2, episodes 6–8: still free, but each one unlocks only after a 30-second ad, for about 60 seconds of content. He says this is deliberately annoying: users learn that the ads are bad enough that paying to skip them feels like an upgrade.
    - Stage 3, episode 9 onwards: coin paywalls placed at cliffhanger moments (the betrayal just discovered, the twist just landed), e.g. 52 coins. He calls this "emotional peak pricing". By now paying feels like relief, an escape from the ads.
  - Each stage normalises the next. Users pay with attention before they pay with money.
  - Rule: where possible, don't go from free to paid in one step. Escalate in this order: attention → effort → money.
- Pattern 2, the anti-calculator. The creator calls this "arguably a dark pattern" and covers it because it makes money and shows how pricing psychology works:
  - Every part of the pricing UI is engineered to stop users working out what they spend:
    - episodes cost between 42 and 66 coins, a variable price, so nobody can keep a mental total
    - bundles are 500 coins for $4.99 and 1,100 for $9.99, deliberately awkward ratios, because clean ratios would make the real cost obvious
    - there is no "you spent $X this month" dashboard and no running total of coins spent on a series
    - prices rise the deeper you get into a show
  - Sunk cost does the rest: every unlocked episode makes quitting feel more expensive, even though the money is already gone.
  - Nobody would pay $40 up front for one show. But 52 coins at a time, about 80 times, adds up to around $40, roughly three times a monthly Netflix subscription, before the user ever sees that number (creator's claim, unverified). Each microtransaction is too small to feel like a decision.
  - He describes it as information architecture that strips out every means of deciding rationally what to spend.
  - The ethical version he gives: be transparent about running totals and let users see what they have spent.
  - A neutral observation he adds: any currency abstraction makes usage-based pricing feel lighter than raw dollars (OpenAI tokens, Vercel compute units, AWS instance hours). That isn't deception in itself. The line is whether you help users stay informed or actively stop them working it out.
  - Rule: if you price in credits, tokens or units, exchange-rate design and spending visibility matter as much as the pricing concept itself.
- Pattern 3, the zero-decision player, which he calls entirely clean:
  - Netflix on a phone has at least six decision points between opening the app and watching: browse, scroll rows, pick, maybe watch a trailer, press play, rotate the phone, perhaps tap to dismiss controls. Each one is a moment where the user might close the app.
  - ReelShort: open the app and content plays. That's it.
  - On the player screen:
    - no visible episode list
    - swipe up = next episode, the primary and only navigation
    - no progress bar to scrub, so you watch linearly with no skipping
    - no "Are you still watching?" interruption
    - no device rotation
  - The same principle, momentum by removing choice, appears in YouTube autoplay, Spotify continuous play and TikTok's swipe-to-next.
  - It only works because of the format. With 60-second vertical episodes a progress bar isn't crucial, an episode list isn't necessary, and vertical shooting removes rotation. The format removes the need for the UI that would otherwise create decision points.
  - Rule: list every decision point in the core flow; each is a spot where the user could drop off. Remove those that add no value: every unneeded tap, every extra screen, and every "Are you sure?" that doesn't protect against a real mistake.

### Recipes
1. Graduated gate, as narrated for ReelShort [01:33–03:37] (episode counts and prices stated; no UI visuals stated)
   1. Open straight into content: no account, sign-up or email before the first play.
   2. Free tier: episodes 1–5 play instantly.
   3. Attention tier: episodes 6–8 unlock behind a 30-second ad each.
   4. Money tier: episode 9 onwards unlocks with coins, and the gate sits at the story's cliffhanger moments.
   5. Ethical adaptation (derived from his rule): keep the attention → effort → money escalation, but don't time the money gate to an engineered emotional peak, and don't make the middle tier deliberately unpleasant.
2. Coin-pricing UI, as narrated (to recognise and avoid, not to copy) [04:08–06:39]
   1. What ReelShort does: variable per-episode prices (42–66 coins), awkward bundle ratios (500 for $4.99, 1,100 for $9.99), prices that rise later in a series, and no spend history or running total.
   2. The ethical inversion he names: show running totals, so users can see what they have spent.
   3. Further inversion, derived from his "spending visibility" rule: show the real-currency equivalent next to every coin price, keep per-unit prices stable, and show the total for this series so far.
3. Zero-decision player, as narrated for ReelShort [08:13–10:18]
   1. Launch straight into playback.
   2. Full-screen vertical video with swipe-up = next episode as the only navigation.

## How To Scientifically Design Addictive Apps
- source: Tim Gabe · https://youtu.be/yBpv5rZoBjA
- type: product-psychology
- tags: gamification, retention, habit, psychology, states, dark-patterns

### Takeaways
- The creator says up front that these mechanisms are morally complicated. They can build better habits or trap people in loops they didn't sign up for. He shows the architecture and leaves the use to the builder.
- Framing: most founders think gamification means decoration (streak counters, badges, leaderboards). In his three examples the gamification is the product.
- Retention context: the average app keeps 7% of users after 30 days, while the apps covered reach up to 90% annual retention (creator's claim, unverified).
- Three mechanisms that stack. The third is what makes the first two stick.
- Mechanism 1, the craving machine (variable-ratio reinforcement):
  - In the 1930s B.F. Skinner rewarded animals on unpredictable schedules: sometimes one press gave food, sometimes ten gave nothing, sometimes one gave a jackpot. That schedule produced the most compulsive behaviour of any reinforcement pattern (creator's claim, unverified).
  - It produces craving, not pleasure: the brain stays in a chase for the next hit. He says it is the same mechanism as slot machines, activating dopamine pathways nearly identical to gambling (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Duolingo's version is mild: XP after every lesson, predictable and pleasant.
  - Finch (self-care pet app) is a stronger version. He cites 14M+ downloads, 675,000 ratings at 4.9 stars on iOS and Apple Editor's Choice (creator's claim, unverified). How it works:
    - you raise a virtual bird by completing real self-care tasks (journaling, breathing exercises, mood check-ins)
    - you pick a destination for the bird from a rotating set, three options for free users and nine for paid
    - the bird adventures there every day, potentially for weeks
    - each location has 15–20 unique discoveries the bird might bring back. Some days are amazing, some are nothing special, and you can't predict which
    - the bird develops personality traits (e.g. confidence, curiosity, resilience) that evolve with how you respond to its adventure stories, and even its likes and dislikes develop in ways you didn't choose. The app says the bird is creating its own personality
    - a profile tracks six traits plus a growth stage, so you never quite know what the bird is becoming
  - He calls Finch gentle about it.
  - League of Legends is the unsubtle extreme. The ranked ladder looks fair (win and climb, lose and drop), but a hidden rating called MMR sends a winning player tougher opponents and a losing player easier matches. The system keeps you near a 50% win rate. Players feel it as random swings (up 100 points one week, down 200 the next, up 150 again), and the algorithm controls the ratio. 130M+ monthly players (creator's claim, unverified).
  - The tell: opening an app not because you need to but just to check.
  - His founder rules:
    1. Keep most of the reward system predictable and transparent, then add a surprise somewhere in it, such as an unexpected bonus reward or a milestone that arrives early.
    2. Don't make everything random. Add controlled surprise to an otherwise transparent system.
    3. Track one system users obsess over (Finch's six-trait profile, League's league points). One visible measure of total progress engages more than 20 scattered badges.
- Mechanism 2, the infinite game (loss aversion + no finish line):
  - People feel a loss about twice as intensely as an equivalent gain (creator's claim, unverified). On day 47 of a streak, the thought of losing it hurts even though it objectively doesn't matter.
  - Duolingo's streak is a single thread: break it and the count is gone. The freeze is smart, but it's still one counter.
  - Freecash's "diamond streak", designed by the creator's agency, goes further:
    - a streak unlocks diamonds: the first at 7 days, another at 42 days
    - missing a day risks losing the diamonds you've accumulated
    - the freeze has to be earned
    - the path isn't infinite, but it is much longer than a weekly reset, and each milestone feels earned and worth protecting
  - The deeper principle: refuse terminal achievement states. The most addictive apps never let you finish.
  - League resets the ranked ladder every season: you climb to platinum and drop back to gold when the season flips. It keeps honour levels and cosmetics through the reset.
  - Peloton has 90% annual subscriber retention, and he credits the community rather than the $1,700 bike (creator's claim, unverified). The engine:
    - live leaderboards ranking watts output against thousands of riders
    - monthly challenges
    - instructors calling out top performers by name
    - cumulative metrics that never cap: total classes, total miles, total output
  - A user at 500 classes doesn't stop, because 600, 700 and 1,000 are all reachable.
  - His founder rules:
    1. Audit the product for done states. If a user can complete your app, retention has a ceiling.
    2. Build streaks that compound into tangible value the user doesn't want to lose, not numbers that simply reset.
    3. If the product has levels, consider periodic resets that force re-engagement while keeping earned status.
- Mechanism 3, the invisible scoreboard (social comparison), which locks the other two in:
  - People check a fitness tracker less for their own steps than to see how they compare with a friend (social comparison theory).
  - Strava has 180M+ athletes on segment leaderboards. In 2025 and early 2026 it deleted 3.9M activities from people gaming the rankings, e.g. uploading e-bike rides as regular rides, with no prize at stake beyond bragging rights (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Peloton adds parasocial bonds: instructors such as Cody Rigsby and Ally Love became celebrities. Users attend to see their instructor and follow their lives, which turns a fitness tool into a community.
  - The moat claim: AI can generate a workout plan or a leaderboard, but it can't replace a human instructor's encouragement. The leaderboard sets up the race; the human bond is what gives it meaning.
  - Why it locks in: without social visibility, a user can quit the craving machine or break a streak privately. Once their progress or tier is visible to others, quitting means publicly admitting they stopped. The social layer turns engagement into identity, and people almost never voluntarily walk away from identity.
  - His founder rules:
    1. Make achievements visible to others. Visibility turns personal goals into status goals.
    2. Build community dynamics, not just features.
    3. Design metrics as a mirror, not a report: stats should make users immediately think about how they compare with others.
- Summary line: it's not what these apps reward but how the reward is structured. The difference between a Duolingo streak and a full progression system is decoration versus architecture.

### Recipes
1. Controlled-surprise reward system [05:43–06:43] (no values stated beyond the examples)
   1. Base layer: a predictable, transparent reward for each core action (e.g. XP per lesson).
   2. Surprise layer: an occasional unannounced bonus, or a milestone that arrives earlier than expected.
   3. One hero progress measure shown prominently instead of many scattered badges.
2. Adventure discovery loop, as narrated for Finch [02:37–04:39]
   1. Real-world self-care tasks feed a virtual pet.
   2. The user picks a destination from a rotating set (three free, nine paid).
   3. The pet explores daily for days or weeks. Each destination holds 15–20 discoveries, revealed unpredictably.
   4. The pet's personality (six traits plus a growth stage) evolves partly from the user's responses to its stories and partly on its own.
3. Compounding streak, as narrated for Freecash [06:43–07:43]
   1. Streak milestones unlock a collectible: a diamond at day 7 and another at day 42.
   2. Missing a day puts the accumulated diamonds at risk.
   3. The streak freeze is earned, not given.
4. Season reset with kept status, as narrated for League of Legends [07:43–08:13, 08:44–09:16]
   1. The competitive rank resets each season (e.g. platinum back to gold).
   2. Earned status markers persist across resets (honour levels, cosmetics).
5. Uncapped cumulative counters, as narrated for Peloton [08:13–08:44]
   1. Show lifetime totals with no cap: classes, miles, output.
   2. Pair them with a live leaderboard (watts), monthly challenges and named instructor call-outs.
6. Invisible scoreboard [09:48–13:25]
   1. Make tiers and achievements visible to other users.
   2. Frame personal stats relative to others ("mirror, not report").
   3. Add human figures and community spaces around the mechanics.

### Gotchas
- Mistaking decoration (streak counters, badges, leaderboards) for gamification architecture.
- 20 scattered badges instead of one visible measure of total progress.
- A product with a "done" state, if long-term retention is the goal.
- Season resets that wipe earned status along with rank.
- Dark patterns, stated plainly. This video is a compulsion-loop playbook and the creator says so:
  - variable-ratio rewards are slot-machine reinforcement
  - League's hidden MMR quietly manipulates win/loss ratios without telling players, which is deceptive
  - the diamond streak deliberately puts accumulated value at risk to exploit loss aversion
  - "never let them finish" is designed to prevent a natural end point
  - public tiers are designed so quitting feels like public failure
- Ethical application for the skill: use surprise only as an occasional bonus on top of a transparent system, never as the core reward and never on paid draws. Offer pauses, freezes and real end points. Make social visibility opt-in. Prefer the clean alternatives in this batch (Apple's closure rings, Peloton's competence feedback) and batch-04's no-shame tracking (MacroFactor).
- Evidence quality: Freecash's diamond streak is the creator's own client work, and the retention figures are unverified.

### Examples named
- B.F. Skinner: variable-ratio reinforcement experiments (1930s).
- Duolingo: predictable XP and a single-thread streak with a freeze.
- Finch: self-care pet with unpredictable destination discoveries and a six-trait personality.
- League of Legends: hidden MMR near a 50% win rate, seasonal rank resets that keep honour levels and cosmetics.
- Freecash: diamond streak with milestones at 7 and 42 days and an earned freeze (creator's client).
- Peloton: live watts leaderboard, monthly challenges, uncapped totals, celebrity instructors (Cody Rigsby, Ally Love).
- Strava: segment leaderboards, and the deletion of 3.9M gamed activities.

## The 3-Stage Trick Behind Every Addictive App
- source: Tim Gabe · https://youtu.be/uoLpH_20qKw
- type: product-psychology
- tags: micro-interaction, animation, states, psychology, retention, habit, gamification, growth

### Takeaways
- Thesis: what matters is not only what you give the user but how they receive it. As apps converge visually, the moment of receiving a reward is one of the last places left to stand out.
- The research hook: in 1989 neuroscientist Kent Berridge found dopamine is the anticipation chemical, firing when something is about to happen under uncertainty. Hand the reward over flatly and you bypass that system (creator's claim, unverified). So the same reward delivered with ceremony is more likely to build a lasting habit than one delivered flat, because the brain processes it differently.
- Framework, "gift or receipt". Every app has a moment where the user receives something: a match, a report, a score, a streak.
  - Receipt (what most apps do): a milestone triggers a modal saying "Congratulations". Something that could be an event is delivered like a bank statement.
  - Gift: a three-stage reward sequence, anticipation → reveal → celebration.
- Most apps deliver only the reveal, often poorly. He says most of the value sits uncaptured in the anticipation before and the celebration after (creator's claim, unverified).
- Stage 1, the anticipation window:
  - Apple has a room built just for perfecting box opening. The lid is tuned to descend over 2–4 seconds under vacuum resistance. It is multi-sensory: you feel the weight, hear a soft rush of air, then see the device perfectly centred (creator's claim, unverified).
  - The proof it works: people keep the boxes, which have a collector community, unboxing videos with tens of millions of views and a resale market. When the packaging itself becomes collectible, the framework is working.
  - Gameblazers' card packs (the creator's agency client) are the digital version:
    - anticipation: you don't know what's in the pack
    - reveal: cards appear one at a time, each restarting the cycle. All at once is one dopamine event; one by one is many. Each card gets its own visual weight and moment
    - celebration: a rare card makes the screen respond with glow, animation and confirmation that this one is different, which is why you open the next pack
  - Ask of every result moment: is there a window before the reveal where uncertainty can build? Even a brief build-up changes the response.
- Stage 2, the ceremony layer:
  - Robinhood shipped full-screen confetti on completed trades in 2016, and made revealing a free-stock reward feel like a scratch-off lottery ticket. It removed the confetti in March 2021, after Massachusetts regulators concluded it trivialised investing and nudged users to trade often, and in January 2024 paid a $7.5M fine. He says the consent order specifically bans celebratory imagery tied to trading frequency (creator's claim, unverified).
  - His point: a design element powerful enough to need a specific legal prohibition is behavioural, not decorative.
  - The positive case is Freecash (creator's client), where users earn money from surveys and offers and the retention problem is getting them back daily for effortful rewards:
    - earnings arrive through a reward-case unboxing animation rather than a direct deposit. The coins are identical either way, but the animated reveal and the visual weight of the number appearing turn a transaction into an event
    - streaks escalate across the week, building towards a special animated reward chest on the final day
  - Ask of the most transactional moment in the app (payout, result, confirmation): what would it look like with weight?
- Stage 3, the afterglow, which most apps ignore and which he says is where long-term retention lives:
  - Kahneman and Tversky showed that something framed as given, rather than accessed, is valued more. Unwrapping versus viewing primes the brain to treat a result as a gift (creator's attribution, unverified).
  - Spotify Wrapped reached 200M users in 24 hours last year (creator's claim, unverified). The data sits in your account all year, but Spotify gates it, reveals it once a year and wraps it in sequential animated slides. The year-long wait is the anticipation; the share prompt at the end is the afterglow.
  - Most companies copy Wrapped by building data summaries. They miss that Wrapped is a ceremony, not a report.
  - Tinder turns a database event (two right swipes) into a full-screen explosion. The celebration window before the chat prompt is the afterglow. 11 daily opens on average (creator's claim, unverified).
  - Snapchat streaks: 477M daily active users, 30+ opens a day, and a longest streak of over 4,000 days (creator's claim, unverified). The streak is a gift you keep earning, so breaking it feels like losing something you own.
  - The afterglow turns a moment into identity: your Wrapped becomes how you see yourself, a streak becomes something to protect, a collection becomes stored value. Leaving means abandoning an investment.
  - After the reveal, give the user a moment with something to keep: a share prompt, a milestone badge, or a stat they can screenshot. Let the result breathe.
- Recap rules:
  1. Build anticipation before the reveal and give the user a moment to lean forward.
  2. Reveal with ceremony; the same result with weight is perceived as a bigger reward.
  3. Celebrate the outcome and let it become part of identity.
- Most apps already have a gift moment hiding in a report, milestone or result they deliver flat.

### Recipes
1. Gift sequence for any result moment [02:03–02:35, 09:48–10:20] (no durations, easing or haptic patterns stated)
   1. Anticipation: a short build-up state before the result, with uncertainty about what is coming.
   2. Reveal: the result appears with visual, haptic and sometimes audio flair. If there are several items, reveal them one at a time.
   3. Celebration: confetti or a glow, then hold on the result.
   4. Afterglow: end on a share prompt, a milestone badge or a screenshot-able stat before handing over to the next action.
2. Card-pack opening, as narrated for Gameblazers [03:37–05:09]
   1. A sealed pack with unknown contents (the anticipation).
   2. Cards appear one at a time, each with its own visual weight.
   3. A rare card triggers a distinct celebration state: glow, animation and confirmation that it is special.
3. Reward-case payout, as narrated for Freecash [06:11–07:44]
   1. Earnings are delivered in a case or chest that the user opens, not credited silently.
   2. Animate the number appearing so it has visual weight, then celebrate.
   3. Escalate daily streak rewards across the week to a special animated chest on the last day.
4. Afterglow screens, as narrated for Spotify Wrapped and Tinder [08:15–09:17]
   1. Wrapped: hold back data the product already has, release it on a fixed annual date as sequential animated slides, and end on a share prompt.
   2. Tinder: a full-screen celebration for the match event, then the chat prompt.

### Gotchas
- The "receipt" pattern: a plain "Congratulations" modal for a milestone.
- Delivering only the reveal, with no build-up before or moment after.
- Dumping several results at once when revealing them one by one would make several moments.
- Copying Spotify Wrapped as a data summary. The point is the gated, sequential, shareable ceremony.
- Ending a result screen without a share prompt, badge or screenshot-able stat.
- Ethics, stated plainly: the Robinhood case shows that ceremony on an action the user repeats against their own interest (trading, betting, spending) gets regulated. Never attach celebration to the frequency of a risky or costly action. Use ceremony for the user's achievements and outcomes, not for the transaction that makes the company money.
- Ethics: anticipation built on uncertain outcomes is the same lever as a slot machine (see "How To Scientifically Design Addictive Apps" above). If the pack or chest is bought with money, it is a loot box. Snapchat's "breaking feels like losing something you own" is loss aversion, which the gamification video above flags as the streak trap that regulators are targeting.
